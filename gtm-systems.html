Enterprise GTM · Operating Philosophy

How I build enterprise GTM systems.

Complex enterprise growth rarely breaks because one department is completely broken. It breaks between discovery, deal strategy, Solutions, Product, Delivery, and customer outcomes. My work increasingly focuses on those seams.

The goal is not more process. It is better judgment, carried further through the organization.
The thesis

Good judgment should compound.

Enterprise deals create valuable customer intelligence, but organizations are often poor at preserving it. Discovery gets trapped in notes. Product feedback becomes a list of requests. Demo strategy lives in individual reps. Implementation lessons arrive too late to influence the next deal.

I think the GTM system should connect those moments. Understand the customer, turn that understanding into a buying thesis, demonstrate the right future state, prove it with relevant evidence, deliver something that can actually work, then feed the lessons back into Product and the next sales cycle.

Diagnose the seams

Look at where information, ownership, expectations, or judgment degrade between functions.

Standardize structure

Make repeatable work easier without removing the judgment required for complex selling.

Sell for production

Design the buying process around what can be operationalized, adopted, measured, and expanded.

Meet the buyer at the maturity of the decision.

The most advanced product story is not automatically the strongest sales story. Determine what the customer needs to believe now, prove that clearly, then expand the vision from there.

The strategy layer

Evidence → Judgment → Action.

The Deal Thesis layer closes the gap that used to sit between discovery and demonstration. The same customer + initiative workspace now holds both the operating evidence and the commercial interpretation.

That means the team can make explicit what it believes the customer is actually buying, which beliefs must be established, what proof should come first, what risks could break the thesis, and what should intentionally stay out of the story.

Evidence

Facts, hypotheses, unknowns, and source context stay visible.

Judgment

The seller forms the thesis: problem, stakes, buyer belief, and structural advantage.

Action

The thesis becomes a proof sequence, next move, and demo strategy context.

How I think about the system

Start where the promise meets reality.

If enterprise revenue mattered and Sales, Solutions, Product, and Delivery were not working together well enough, I would start with Delivery. That is where the commercial promise meets operational reality.

I would review recent enterprise engagements and ask what Delivery received, what they had to rediscover, what was difficult to implement, what had been overpromised, and what represented a real product gap. Then I would trace those problems backward through the system rather than assuming any one function was the cause.

Information gap

The solution was viable, but context never survived the handoff.

Solution-design gap

The need was understood, but the proposed approach was not operationally sound.

Expectation gap

The product could solve the problem, but the customer believed they bought something different.

Product gap

The desired outcome genuinely requires capability that does not exist.

Product feedback

Translate requests into operating problems.

Customers often describe the solution they think they need. I try to identify the operating problem underneath the request, determine whether it is isolated or repeated, and frame the change in terms of customer value and commercial impact.

That has led me to think in systems rather than isolated features: planning and workload visibility when administration becomes too complex, governance and approvals when communications teams need enterprise controls, integration layers when IT dependency slows deployment, and decision support when reporting needs to become action.

First 90 days

What I would want to know before I tried to fix anything.

I would not begin with a reorganization or a new methodology. I would build a fact base around where the enterprise motion actually degrades and where good outcomes currently depend on heroics.

Days 0–30

Trace the work

  • Review recent enterprise wins, difficult implementations, expansions, and problem accounts.
  • Interview Delivery first, then Solutions, Sales, Product, and Customer Success.
  • Map what was known in discovery versus what had to be rediscovered after signature.
  • Separate information, expectation, solution-design, and product gaps.
Days 31–60

Find the patterns

  • Identify repeated failure points across the customer lifecycle.
  • Determine which problems are process, product, enablement, or ownership issues.
  • Find where the best teams already work differently.
  • Define a small number of changes with measurable commercial impact.
Days 61–90

Close the loop

  • Pilot improvements to discovery, handoff, solution validation, or product-feedback loops.
  • Create clear ownership for recurring cross-functional decisions.
  • Instrument the system with a small set of meaningful GTM and delivery measures.
  • Turn early wins and lessons into reusable operating patterns.

Questions I would want answered

Where does Delivery most often say, “we did not know that before signature”?
Which enterprise deals require heroics to get from sold to live?
What does Solutions repeatedly discover that Sales or Product does not see?
Which customer asks are actually repeated operating patterns rather than one-off features?
Where do expectations diverge between what was sold, what was demonstrated, and what can be delivered?
Which handoffs create the most rework, delay, or loss of customer confidence?
What successful implementation patterns are not yet part of how we sell?
Which GTM decisions lack a clear owner today?
